Prediction-market microstructure · Research report · October 2026

Kalshi Edge Atlas

Where can a retail quant still make money on an exchange priced by professional capital? We tested seven hypotheses against 6.5 million real Kalshi trades, end-game candles from 9,452 markets, and 2,351 baseball games aligned minute by minute with Polymarket.

Summary

Six hypotheses that depend on knowing more than the market, or on the market mispricing something, failed. A machine-learning model lost to the closing price. Market making in the major leagues earns less spread than it pays in fees. A longshot bias that looked like p < 0.0001 disappeared under a larger, cleaner test. Polymarket follows Kalshi, not the other way around.

The one source of positive expectancy that held up is the exchange's own liquidity subsidy, the Liquidity Incentive Program. It pays for resting orders whether or not they trade. We implemented the official scoring rules, built a quoting bot, and ran four 8-hour shadow tests against live order books. All four finished net positive, but once inventory losses are counted the diversified runs earned well under 1% of capital per day, and reward share is an upper bound.

  • Out of sample. Parameters, league selection and time anchors are fit on the first half of the data (by settlement date) and evaluated on the second half.
  • Clustered inference. Every trade in a market shares one settlement outcome, so t-statistics are computed across markets, not trades. Calibration claims use exact binomial tests.
  • No look-ahead. End-game windows are anchored to expected_expiration_time, which Kalshi publishes when the market opens, plus a league offset estimated on the first half only. Settlement time is never used to select trades.
03 · Information edge

The closing price knows the game better than the model

The starting point was an XGBoost win-probability model for the NBA: 538-style Elo, rolling offensive and defensive ratings over 5, 10 and 20 games, rest and back-to-back flags, and later a player-availability feature that prices each missing rotation player by recent Game Score. The model was trained on 2019–24, calibrated with isotonic regression on 2024–25, and scored out of sample on 1,068 games of the 2025–26 season against the real Kalshi price four hours before tip-off.

The market's log loss was 0.5685 against the model's 0.6099. Adding injury information brought the model to 0.5940, and that version was allowed to see who actually sat out that night. In a logistic regression on both forecasts, the model's coefficient was 0.004: everything it knew was already in the price. A sweep of 107 combinations of timing, edge threshold and price band found no cell with t > 1.65, where chance alone would produce about five.

04 · Market making

The spread exists, but fees and adverse selection consume it

Each trade has a passive counterparty. Treating that maker as a market maker, we measured the realized spread: the profit on the fill marked against the volume-weighted price of trades 10 seconds later. In the major leagues, a 1¢ quoted spread leaves the maker with a few hundredths of a cent, while Kalshi charges those series a maker fee several times larger.

Two-leg arbitrage between the two teams' contracts in the same NBA game fared no better: of 82,337 pre-game hourly snapshots, 62 were profitable after both legs' fees, and those are most likely candles that closed a few seconds apart.

05 · Methodology

A false discovery at p < 0.0001

The most attractive signal appeared in the final minutes of games. Longshots priced 2–10¢ reversed in only 11 of 572 markets in the trade sample, where their prices implied 31.5. Selling those longshots passively earned 3.6¢ per contract out of sample, with t = 5.4. We did not trade on it. Three checks followed.

  1. A sampling bias. Each time window kept only its most recent 1,000 trades. A miracle comeback triggers a burst of volume that pushes the longshot trades before it out of the sample, so the sample under-counts longshots that went on to win. We re-fetched all 1,242 truncated windows in full. The signal survived.

  2. Sampling that ignores volume. We took a snapshot every five minutes of wall-clock time inside the ex-ante end-game window and expanded to 9,452 markets. Longshots on both sides were priced correctly.

  3. The source of the anomaly. Running the snapshot test on the same markets as the trade sample reproduced the low reversal rate. The anomaly belongs to those particular markets' outcomes, not to a mispricing at the moments retail traders buy. After this many cuts of the data, an outlier of that size is what chance produces.

06 · Cross-venue

During games, Polymarket follows Kalshi

We aligned Kalshi and Polymarket prices minute by minute for 2,351 MLB games and regressed each venue's next move on the gap between them. In play, Polymarket closes 92% of the gap within one minute while Kalshi barely moves toward Polymarket. Using Polymarket as a signal to trade Kalshi produced no configuration with in-sample t > 1, and every candidate lost money out of sample. Kalshi is where in-game price discovery happens.

07 · The survivor

The exchange pays for liquidity, and that payment needs no forecast

Kalshi's Liquidity Incentive Program photographs each eligible order book once per second. On each side, it walks down from the best bid and admits whole price levels until resting size reaches the market's Target Size. Orders at or above the reference price score their full size; each tick below it multiplies the score by the Discount Factor. A participant's reward is their share of all qualifying score, averaged over the period, times the period's prize.

score(order) = size × DF^(ticks below reference price) share(user) = ½ · Σ score(yes bids)/Σ score(all yes) + ½ · Σ score(no bids)/Σ score(all no) payout(user) = time-averaged share × period reward × (qualifying snapshots ÷ all snapshots) Typical terms : Target Size 1,000 contracts · DF 0.5 · reward $10–$1,000 per market per day

Programs running from August to December 2026 total about $13.4M. When we scanned, 6,657 programs were live with $775k in prizes, mostly outside sports: state gas prices, weather, streaming and box-office counts, political mentions. About half of these markets are already crowded, with a 1¢ spread and a median depth of 13,634 contracts per side. The other half have spreads of 2¢ or more, where a Target-Size order placed inside the spread takes most of the score, at the cost of becoming the quote that gets hit first.

Our first scoring implementation cut off orders at the back of the queue within a price level. The program terms admit the whole level, and correcting that raised the median share for joining the best price in a 1¢ market from zero to 58%.

08 · Forward test

Four 8-hour shadow runs on live order books

Every 30 seconds the bot read the real book and real trades for 30 markets. It scored its hypothetical quotes with the program rules and accrued reward over time. Any real taker trade at or through a hypothetical quote counted as a fill at our price, and inventory was marked to the mid. No order was ever sent.

Cumulative net P&L: reward plus inventory marked to mid

v1 selects by reward per dollar of capital. v2 adds diversification by Kalshi category (at most 6 per category, 2 per series), a per-market stop that pulls quotes once inventory losses exceed max($10, 2× reward earned), and excludes markets settling within six hours of the test's end.

How to read this. The v1 runs look strongest, but their 30 markets were almost all daily AAA gas-price contracts. Kalshi lists each state as its own series, so the per-series cap did not diversify anything. Their positive markout means the inventory happened to move our way: that is an unhedged bet on gasoline prices, not a property of the strategy. In the v1 join-best run, a single Missouri market lost $153 in its first two hours.

The diversified v2 runs are the better guide. Fills there lost about $100–$200 within 30 minutes of execution, which is adverse selection: people who trade with a resting quote often know something. That cost consumed most of the reward and left about $60–$75 of net profit per run, roughly 0.2% of capital over eight hours. The edge is real but thin, and it depends on a reward share that real competitors would contest.

09 · Strategy specification

Subsidy Harvester

Market selection

Live incentive markets with spread ≤ 20¢, where trades in the last 24 hours that would have hit our quote total less than 25% of our size, and whose program outlasts the holding period. Rank by reward per dollar of capital and diversify by category and by underlying.

Quoting

Rest Target Size on both sides. With a spread of 3¢ or more, improve each side by one tick to lead the queue; otherwise join the best price. A filled YES and a filled NO together lock in $1 and are netted immediately.

Risk

Net inventory per side capped at Target Size. Per-market stop on inventory loss. Pull quotes ahead of settlement and known data releases. Skip near-empty books where resting at extreme prices could be judged contrary to the program's purpose.

Execution

Orders go through POST /portfolio/events/orders with client order IDs for idempotency. Run the bot against Kalshi's demo environment before committing any capital.

10 · Limitations

What this report does not show

  • Reward share is an upper bound. Shadow quotes are invisible to other participants. Real quotes would draw competitors who add size, and the equilibrium share can only be measured with real orders.
  • Eight hours is a short sample. The tests saw few scheduled information events. Tail losses around data releases, such as the morning AAA update, need a longer window.
  • Marks are noisy. In thin books the mid can jump by several cents when a single order appears or disappears, so the inventory marks are noisy until the markets settle.
  • The program can change. Kalshi sets the terms unilaterally, can modify or end programs at any time, pays after each period, and does not pay amounts under $1.
  • Nothing here involved a live trade, and nothing here is investment advice.
11 · Next steps

What would settle the question

  1. Re-mark the shadow inventories at settlement prices once the markets resolve, replacing the mid-price marks.

  2. Run the v2 bot in Kalshi's demo environment to validate order handling, then with small real size to measure the reward share that survives competition.

  3. Add event-aware quoting: pull quotes before each market's known information releases, and hedge correlated inventory across related markets such as neighboring gas-price strikes.

  4. Reverse the cross-venue finding: test whether Kalshi's lead predicts Polymarket's price, using Polymarket trade data for execution.
